Correction, 21 September 2026. As first published, this note led with the spread in suite means across eight retrainings of one recipe, and included a table of per-skill rates for two individual retrains. Both have been removed.

Those figures are measurements of how much retraining alone moves a result. That quantity belongs to a separate paper by the same author, currently under peer review, and an internal register written on 19 September — after this note was published — reserves it for that paper and permits only rejection rates and calibration to be published from these panels.

Nothing here was withdrawn because it was wrong. The argument below is unchanged and now rests on the rejection rates, which is what the register allows and, as it turns out, the sharper way to make the point. The original version is retained privately.

A regression rule that fired on 81% of releases where nothing had changed

Rahil Lasne · reproducible from the sample on the release check page

If you train a robot policy twice with the same data, the same config and a different seed, you get two different robots. Everyone who has done it knows this. What is less discussed is what it does to the number you use to decide whether the new model ships.

We trained π0.5 eight times on LIBERO-Object with identical data and settings, only the training seed changed, and evaluated each retrain on 1,000 episodes: ten tasks, 100 episodes each, the same initial states for every retrain. Because every run came from one recipe, any two of them differ by exactly nothing, and every difference a test reports between them is an error we can identify as an error.

What a regression rule does on that data

Pick any two of those retrains, call one "last release" and the other "this release", and run the rule almost everyone runs: flag any skill whose drop clears p ≤ 0.05. On this battery that rule fired on 81% of comparisons where nothing had changed. The priced rule — which accounts for the fact that training is itself a source of variation — fired on 2.5%.

Both numbers are error rates. There was no difference to find in any of those comparisons, so every firing was a false alarm. A rule that cries wolf four times out of five is not a safety net; it is noise that people learn to ignore, which is worse than having no rule.

The suite mean has the opposite failure. Averaged over ten skills, movement on any one of them is diluted to almost nothing, so a release can look flat while the one skill a particular customer runs all day has moved underneath it. Reporting the average alone is not a conservative choice; it is a choice to not look.

What the check can and cannot say

At 100 episodes per skill, the smallest drop this design can call real is about 12 points. Anything smaller is real-looking and not separable from noise, and saying so is the whole job: the check reports "suspect" together with the number of episodes it would take to know, rather than flagging everything or passing everything.

The one thing no two-version comparison can tell you is whether a skill is down because the model is worse or because training is a lottery and you drew a different ticket. Those two look identical from the outside. Separating them needs the same recipe trained more than once. How much that buys is a measurement this note does not report.

What to do with your own logs

  • Report success per skill, with the interval, not only the suite mean. Ten episodes per task gives an interval 40 to 50 points wide; say so next to the number.
  • Evaluate consecutive versions on the same initial states. The comparison becomes paired and the interval roughly halves for free.
  • Put the episodes where the decision is. Ten skills at 100 episodes resolves a 12-point drop; the same 1,000 episodes on the three skills that matter resolves 7.
  • If a release decision matters, train the recipe more than once and pick per skill. It is the only way to know which of the two stories you are in.

The check that produced the table runs in your browser on a CSV with four columns: version, skill, episode, success. Nothing is uploaded. The sample above is one click.

Scope

One policy class, one simulated suite, ten tasks, 100 episodes each, eight seeds. The size of the effect is a property of this cell; on a different suite the same method read a gain of 1.7 points with an interval that crossed zero. What carries is the shape: a suite average shrinks with the number of tasks while every deployed skill keeps its full swing, so per-skill regressions are hidden by construction, not by bad luck. The statistics are ordinary, Wilson and Newcombe intervals with a stated floor, and the code is open source: pip install orbit-eval, then orbit-eval release evals.csv.
