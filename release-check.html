A release field is not a release ledger

Every automated-driving incident filed with the US federal regulator has a field for the software version that was running. The field is filled in almost everywhere and usable almost nowhere.

NHTSA republishes this file monthly and overwrites it in place, so every figure below is pinned to that hash. The snapshot and the analysis are published with this page.

When an automated-driving company ships new software, its vehicles behave differently the next day. Anyone outside that company who wants to know whether the change helped has one public place to look. Under a federal Standing General Order, named manufacturers and operators must file a report on certain crashes involving their automated-driving systems, and each report carries a field naming the software version that was running. The field is called Automation Feature Version. It is the only routinely published, legally compelled record anywhere of which robot software was deployed when something went wrong.

We took one snapshot of that record and asked whether an outside analyst can use it to identify the deployed release, and then judge whether a before-and-after comparison is supported.

Two terms are worth fixing before the numbers. A release is one immutable version of the deployed software. An arm is the set of incidents recorded while a single release was live, which is what a before-and-after comparison needs two of.

The field is filled in, which is not the same as analysable

Reports get revised, so we kept the highest report version for each report identifier, removing 41 superseded revisions. Incidents can be filed more than once, so we then collapsed records sharing an incident identifier, removing one more. Nothing below depends on those two steps: the central comparison moves from a Fisher exact p of 0.059 before deduplication to 0.061 after.

Zoox illustrates the failure that waiting cannot fix. If every incident receives its own build string, the release arms have size one by construction, and no quantity of further reports will produce a comparison between two Zoox releases.

Normalisation needs a human, and the reason is arithmetic

Waymo's 1,211 reports carry 13 distinct raw strings. Merging on formatting alone, meaning case and punctuation, collapses them to nine. Getting from nine to five requires someone to make judgment calls. The formatting merges are safe and automatic: one release absorbs a variant with the comma missing, a variant with two spaces instead of one, and a variant with no space after the comma.

The remaining cases cannot be automated, and the reason is worth stating plainly. We flagged 44 near-duplicate pairs across all operators and merged none of them, because a one-character difference is exactly how Version 10 differs from Version 11. Merging on edit distance would destroy the releases the analysis exists to recover. Exactly one of those 44 pairs is a pure text variant, a misspelling of Generation. The decisive case is a report filed under 35th Generation ADS, Version 10. Its digits differ from its neighbour's, so any automated rule must keep it as a separate release. Only a person who knows that Waymo has no 35th-generation vehicle can resolve it.

A ledger is therefore a field, plus a normalisation step, plus a named person who signed the judgment calls, plus the original strings kept alongside the canonical ones.

The one comparison that exists does not conclude

Version 10's reports from June 2025 to April 2026 and Version 11's from June to July 2026 give 875 and 186 incidents. Across four outcome measures, nothing separates them.

Differences in percentage points. The Release Record for the same two versions counts all of their months and leaves the Version 10 reports filed under near-miss labels for a person to merge, so its arms hold 900 and 274 reports and its figures differ from these.

The measure that looks alarming is moderate-or-worse injury, which rises from 0.91 to 2.69 percent. That is a tripling, and it rests on five events.

Moderate-or-worse injury, at these two arm sizes

2.49pp smallest detectable difference, against 1.77pp observed.

Given these arm sizes and the injury rate of the two arms pooled, the smallest difference detectable at 80 percent power and a 5 percent level is 2.49 points. The observed difference is smaller than that. The limit uses a rate read off the outcome, because nobody stated the rate the comparison was designed around, so by this tool's own rule the comparison is indeterminate, not incapable.

For every other operator the position is worse by an order of magnitude. On an outcome occurring in one percent of incidents, Avride's two largest arms could detect a 9.2-point difference, May Mobility's a 27.9-point difference, and Zoox's a 39.4-point difference. Their releases would have to make a rare outcome ten to forty times more common before this record could show it.

What this does not establish

The record carries no miles, no operating hours, no trips and no fleet size. Without exposure there is no rate.

No rates

Nothing here is a per-mile number and none can be derived. The record has no exposure denominator.

No ranking

Operators differ in fleet size, geography, operating domain and internal review practice, so these counts are not comparable across them.

No causal claim

Nothing here shows that a release changed an outcome. Fleet size and operating area both grew across the window, the two arms are eleven months and two months long, and they sit a year apart.

No safety verdict

The subject is the evidentiary record, not the machines. This is not a safety certification and it does not stand in for a regulator, a notified body, or a machinery-safety assessment.

No claim about demand

The version field exists here because a federal order required it. That says nothing about whether an operator outside a regulated industry would keep such a record, or pay to have it checked.

What it establishes

Compelled version reporting produces complete field coverage and 95.3 percent publicly observable values. That raw field still needs normalisation and named human adjudication before it can be joined, thirteen strings reducing to five releases for a single operator. Release transitions are reconstructable from public records alone. Sixteen of seventeen operators file a version field that cannot support a before-and-after comparison, because it is too coarse, too fine, or withheld. And the one record that can support arms produces a comparison whose detection limit is larger than the effect under discussion.

The last of those is the general result. A record can exist, be complete, be legally compelled, and still be unable to answer the question it appears to answer.

The first version of this tool was wrong, in the flattering direction

The checks on this page were then turned on a second real corpus: twenty published robot-policy claims with citations, from other groups. The tool reported five of them as supported. A calibrated audit of the same twenty says none survive.

The tool was wrong, and the way it was wrong is worth stating. It computed its interval from episode-level sampling noise alone. When the two arms being compared are two separate training runs, the unit of replication is the run, and run-to-run variation is invisible to episode-level noise, so the interval was far too narrow. Once the unit of replication was enforced, all twenty claims read insufficient evidence, which agrees with the calibrated result.

Two smaller faults surfaced the same way. A significant result from a study too small to reliably detect the claimed effect was being reported as support, which is precisely the case where effect sizes inflate and replication fails. And the detection limit was computed from the observed pooled rate, so looking at the outcome could move a verdict that is supposed to precede it.

All three are one failure in different clothes: an instrument that flatters the claim in front of it. Finding them in our own tool is the only reason to believe the rest of this page.
