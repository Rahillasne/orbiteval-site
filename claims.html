Claim Check · protocol v1

Twenty published claims that a robot policy got better. We checked all twenty.

None of them is independently supported. Not because the researchers were careless, but because the number of episodes behind a robot result is almost never enough to tell an improvement from the noise of training the same recipe twice.

Corrected 25 September 2026. A second reading of the papers found that 8 of the 20 claims do not state one episode count per arm, so no detection limit is computed for them. They are now marked “count not stated” instead of being given a count, and each comparison names what it was measured against. Five stated counts were also corrected against the papers, which moved two claims from not supported to inconclusive; claims failing on episode count alone fell from 11 to 7. None is supported, before or after. The previous corpus stays published.

What this evidence is, and what it is not.

Provenance

Published claims, mostly from simulators.

Each row is a comparison an external group published, taken from their own table. The column marked venue says whether the episodes behind it ran on a physical robot or in a simulation benchmark, and says unstated whenever the claim itself does not make that clear.

Not a fraud finding

"Not supported" is not "false".

A claim fails this check when the evidence behind it cannot separate the reported gain from noise. The gain may well be real. Nobody, including the authors, can currently tell from the numbers reported.

Pre-registered

The rule was fixed before the corpus.

The threshold each claim is measured against is derived from external sources at run time, never typed in by hand. An earlier version of this audit typed three numbers and they drifted. That is the whole reason this page exists.

Why a small gain cannot be trusted.

Train the same recipe twice, change nothing but the seed, and the two runs do not score the same. So we built the case where the right answer is known: every comparison below is between two policies trained from an identical recipe, where the true difference is zero by construction.

Then we ran the test almost everyone runs — pool the episodes, compare the two rates — and counted how often it announced a winner.

How a claim is checked.

Step 1

Take the gain the authors report, the two success rates they print, and the number of episodes per arm. If the paper does not state one episode count, stop: the claim is listed as count not stated. Nothing else from the paper is used.

Step 2

Compute the smallest amount of retraining noise that would be enough to erase that gain. This is a property of the claim alone, written σ* below.

Step 3

Compare σ* against how much retraining noise other groups have actually measured, scaled to the success rate this particular claim sits at, because noise on a proportion is smaller near 100% than near 50%.

Step 4

Report one of four states, and never force a claim into better or worse. If eight external measurements do not pin the noise down to a single number, the honest verdict is the range, and the range is what gets printed.

The limits of this page.

Read this before you quote it

This is benchmark evidence, not warehouse evidence. Nineteen of the twenty claims rest on simulation benchmarks. Nothing here measures a robot picking groceries, and no rate on this page should be quoted as the failure rate of any deployed system.

The null reference is a reference class, not a forecast. It says how often a standard test announces a winner when the true difference is zero, in a particular family of policies and tasks we could run repeatedly. It is the right starting point for a deployment and the wrong finishing point. We replace it with your own repeated measurements as soon as there are any.

The sample is what we could verify, not the literature. 41 papers were screened to find claims reporting enough detail to check at all. Eight of the twenty turned out, on a line-by-line reading, not to state one episode count, so no detection limit is computed for them; they stay on the page rather than being dropped after the fact. This is not a random sample of published work.
